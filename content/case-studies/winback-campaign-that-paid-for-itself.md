The brief was to build a winback campaign for a fitness app with around 140,000 lapsed subscribers. The previous attempt had been a single email with a 50% discount, sent to everyone who had ever cancelled, twice a year. It converted at 0.4% and, once the discounted months were counted, lost money.

This is a draft article used to check the blog build. The numbers are illustrative.

## Start with the exit, not the offer

Everyone in that list of 140,000 had left for a reason, and the reasons were already in the data — the cancellation survey, the last session before the lapse, whether they had ever completed a workout at all.

Three groups did most of the work:

- **Never activated.** Signed up, never finished a first session. About 44% of the list.
- **Used it, then stopped.** A real habit that broke, usually around a holiday or an injury.
- **Priced out.** Cancelled within a week of a price change or a failed payment.

A discount is only the right answer for the third group. For the first, a discount is a cheaper version of a thing they never learned to use.

## The three sequences

Each group got a different sequence, and only the third one mentioned price at all.

![The retention view the sequences were built from](/ui-analytics.webp "The cohort view the three sequences were built from.")

For the never-activated group, the email did one thing: it offered the fifteen-minute session, by name, that people who did stay had completed first.

## Results after ten weeks

| Group | Size | Reactivated | Still active at day 60 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Never activated | 61,000 | 2.1% | 71% |
| Habit broke | 48,000 | 5.4% | 66% |
| Priced out | 31,000 | 3.8% | 52% |

The blended rate — 3.6% — is nine times the old campaign, but the number that matters is the last column. The people who came back for a reason other than price mostly stayed.

## What to take from this

A winback campaign is a segmentation problem wearing an offer's clothes. Write the segments first; the offer is the last decision, not the first.
